On May 17, 2009, a little game called Minecraft crawled out of his cave and landed on the TigSource forum, blinking in the harsh light of the internet for the first time. The early feedback was a goldmine, though, helping Notch turn what was basically a glorified block simulator into the first draft of your childhood addiction. Suddenly, the world of Minecraft wasn't just a flat surface of sadness. It had hills, trees, and lakes. Players got their hands on blocks like dirt, stone, and planks to unleash their inner architect, or a place to hide from all their real-world responsibilities. Oh, and multiplayer? On December 23rd, 2009, Minecraft hit puberty and entered its third phase with the in-development version. This was the moment where Mojang decided, alright, time to make some money, and introduced the first paid version of the game. For a few bucks, you got tools, weapons, and the ability to gather and cook food like you were prepping for doomsday. Players could also utilize crafting and smelting, and there was also a day and night cycle to keep you slightly paranoid. In 2010, Minecraft decided to take a sledgehammer to the idea of boundaries with InfDev, which stands for infinite development. Why infinite? Because they thought, why not make the world bigger than your ego after winning a game of Mario Kart? The map could now expand forever, procedurally generating new terrain until your computer begs for mercy. During this phase, the game got a serious upgrade. We're talking 3D clouds that would make Bob Ross cry, new craftable items, and cave systems so deep they'd give Indiana Jones nightmares. Fun fact, the Minecraft map is a colossal four billion square kilometers. To put that into perspective, Earth, the blue ball we live on, only has 510 million square kilometers.
